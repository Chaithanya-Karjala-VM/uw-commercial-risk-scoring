01 overview

The mental model

One sentence: an underwriter fills in a submission, and the pipeline turns it into four scores — how much it's likely to cost in claims, how likely it is to actually bind, whether it fits the book's appetite and capacity, and one blended number that drives the tier and the rate.

There is no single model. There are six trained statistical models and two hand-built rules engines, chained together:

2 GLMs
Severity
2 GLM/GBM
Large-loss
2 classifiers
Bind
rules engine

rules engine

"2" for frequency/severity/large-loss because each is trained twice — once for property lines (Commercial Property, BOP, Inland Marine), once for liability lines (Workers Comp, General Liability, Professional Liability, Umbrella/Excess, Commercial Auto). Property and liability risk are rated on genuinely different logic (a building burning down vs. an employee getting hurt), so they were never pooled into one model.

The four scores an underwriter actually sees:

  • Loss Propensity Score (0=best, 100=worst) — output of the three ML models, blended into a predicted loss ratio.
  • Bind Propensity Score (0–100, higher=more likely to bind) — a rules-based formula, not a trained model.
  • Appetite/Capacity Score (0–100) — a rules-based formula with a hard veto gate.
  • Composite UW Score (0–100) — a fixed weighted blend of the three above, which sets the risk tier.
Why rules, not models, for Bind and Appetite

The source file has no historical "did this submission actually bind" outcome column, and Appetite is fundamentally a policy decision (sanctions lists, concentration caps, capacity treaties), not a pattern to be learned from data. Both were rebuilt as real formulas — reverse-engineered by regressing against the file's own stored scores — rather than force-fit into a model that would just be learning noise. See §9 and §10.

04 lob_config.py

Features, and keeping the future out of the past

The single most important rule in the whole pipeline: a model can only see what an underwriter would actually know at the moment of quoting — never anything that only exists because a claim already happened.

What's excluded, and why

CLAIM_OUTCOME_COLS is a hard exclusion list applied to every feature set, no exceptions: claim counts, claim severity, incurred amounts, large-claim flags, litigation flags, hospital visit counts — anything that only exists after a claim. These are the targets models predict, never inputs.

Verified, not assumed

claims_free_years looks like it could be leakage (a field that's suspiciously perfect at predicting claims), so it was checked directly: among rows that do have a current-period claim, is claims_free_years still often positive? Yes — confirmed genuinely backward-looking, safe to use. data_audit.py §4 re-runs this exact check on demand.

Explicit-window history over ambiguous totals

claim_count_last_3_years and large_loss_count_last_5_years are used in place of the file's prior_claim_count / prior_total_incurred_usd pair — the "prior" fields don't specify a window, which makes them impossible to reason about consistently across submissions of different tenure.

"General"-tagged fields pulled in anyway

A handful of fields are tagged General in the dictionary (not Loss) but are genuinely risk-relevant, so they're in the feature sets regardless of their tag: roof_type, num_stories, built_to_current_code_flag, distance to fire station/hydrant, business_tenure_years, revenue_growth_trend_pct, debt_to_equity_ratio, avg_weekly_work_hours (WC-specific).

Found & fixed — year_built redundancy

Correlation with building_age_years: -0.9999999999999999. Feeding both to a model as separate features is pure multicollinearity, and was a real contributor to the erratic, non-monotonic scoring an 8-line-of-business audit turned up. year_built is still a live form field (it's how an underwriter naturally thinks), but it now only feeds building_age_years — it was removed from the model feature list entirely.

One genuinely new field added, and it had to earn its spot

primary_cat_peril_exposure (the dominant catastrophe peril type — Wildfire / Flood-Storm Surge / Earthquake / Wind-Hail / Elevated-Multiple / Low-Minimal) was chi-square tested against the large-claim flag before being added: p = 0.008, genuinely significant. Every other untested categorical field checked the same way (business description, occupancy type alone, industry, water-body type, region) came back p > 0.05 — noise from thin per-category counts on a rare target — and was left out.

Ordinal tiers, encoded to respect their order

Flood/Earthquake/Wildfire/Wind zone tiers arrive as text — Low/Moderate/High/Severe. One-hot encoding would throw away the fact that they're ordered, which breaks monotonic constraints (see §5). encode_ordinal_tiers() maps them to integers 0–3 instead, consistently, everywhere they're used.

State → Region, derived not typed

31 states map to 8 regions with a confirmed clean 1:1 relationship (zero exceptions across all 20,000 rows) — so the live form only asks for State, and Region is derived automatically wherever a model needs it.

05 frequency_model.py

Frequency model

Predicts how many claims a submission is expected to generate — the first of the three inputs that get blended into Loss Propensity.

Target & method

Poisson GLM (sklearn.linear_model.PoissonRegressor) racing a monotonic-constrained gradient-boosted tree (HistGradientBoostingRegressor, Poisson loss). Whichever wins on held-out deviance is what actually gets saved and used — not a fixed choice.

Found & fixed — the exposure-unit collapse

An 8-line-of-business audit found predicted frequency barely moving across the entire real range of headcount or building value — 0.28 at 1 employee vs. 0.18 at 5,000 employees — because both were just ordinary features the model had to learn a relationship for, not treated as an explicit exposure multiplier the way policy term already was. Meanwhile premium scales exactly linearly with both, so loss ratio collapsed toward zero at scale for purely mechanical reasons, regardless of real risk.

Fix: standard actuarial practice — fit a per-unit rate and multiply by the exposure units explicitly, the same way exposure_years (from policy term) already worked. Property's unit is $1M of TIV; liability's is 1 employee. The unit column is removed from that group's own feature list (it becomes a multiplier, not something the model fits a coefficient for).

Monotonic constraints — the real-world direction every field is forced to respect

A GBM left unconstrained will happily fit a wiggly, non-monotonic curve to noise — e.g. "risk goes up, then down, then up again" as building age increases, just because that pattern existed in a few hundred training rows. Every numeric feature gets a monotonic_cst direction, so the tree can only ever move the prediction the real-world-sensible way.

Found & fixed — 76-point swings from unconstrained fields

Before this pass, revenue_growth_trend_pct, business_tenure_years, avg_weekly_work_hours, avg_employee_age and avg_wage_per_employee_usd had no constraint at all. The 8-LOB audit found them swinging the score by up to 76 points via non-monotonic, erratic patterns purely from undertrained noise. All five now carry an explicit, documented direction.

The drop-and-refit safeguard

Even with a monotonic constraint requested, the linear GLM half is fit first and checked: if any coefficient's sign contradicts its declared real-world direction, that field is dropped and the model is refit without it, rather than trusting a contradicted assumption. This fired for 9 property fields (including debt_to_equity_ratio, wind_zone_tier, and — after investigation, correctly — revenue_growth_trend_pct / business_tenure_years whose direction flipped on this particular book) and 2 liability fields on the current retrain.

Re-checked specifically, this session

debt_to_equity_ratio's dropped, backwards-looking sign was tested directly against real claims rather than assumed: significant for Property (p=0.037 / p=0.0075) but the effect is negligible — r≈-0.02 to -0.03, roughly 0.05% of variance, almost certainly confounded (larger, more-established businesses use more leverage and have better risk management). For Liability it isn't even significant (p=0.79 / 0.94). Dropping it is the correct call, not a bug — flipping the constraint to chase a razor-thin, confounded correlation would be fitting noise.

Train / test split — by time, not randomly

Rows are sorted by effective_date and split 75/25 — the model trains only on the earlier 75% and is scored on the later 25% it has never seen, in time order. A random shuffle-split would let the model "see the future" in a loose sense (a submission from the same broker, same week, ends up on both sides of the split); the time-based split is the honest test of whether the model actually generalizes forward.

06 severity_model.py

Severity model

Predicts the expected dollar amount of a claim, given one happens — attritional (ordinary) claims only. Large claims are handled entirely separately (§7), on the reasoning that a fire that destroys a building and a fire that singes a doormat are not draws from the same distribution.

Attritional-only split

Every claim above this file's own peril-specific large_claim_threshold_usd is excluded before fitting — 123 large property claims and 178 large liability claims held out, leaving 2,552 and 3,729 attritional claims respectively to fit the severity curve on.

Gamma GLM vs. monotonic GBM

Same race pattern as frequency — GammaRegressor (Gamma is the standard actuarial distribution for a strictly-positive, right-skewed claim-severity target) against a monotonic HistGradientBoostingRegressor with loss="gamma". Same MONOTONIC_DIRECTION table as frequency, with one difference: employees and TIV stay as ordinary constrained features here rather than becoming an explicit exposure multiplier — a bigger operation plausibly costs more per claim when one happens, even though claim count needed the exposure-unit fix instead.

A known asymmetry, not an oversight

Unlike frequency and large-loss, severity's GLM has no drop-and-refit safeguard for a contradicting coefficient sign — it goes straight to fit-and-compare. The GBM half is still monotonically constrained, so the deployed model (whichever wins) can't misbehave on sign; the GLM alone, if it ever won, could in principle carry an unconstrained coefficient. On the current data GBM wins Property and GLM wins Liability — worth knowing this asymmetry exists if the data changes materially.

07 large_loss_model.py

Large-loss model

Two parts: will a large loss happen (a classifier), and how big is it if it does (Generalized Pareto, an extreme-value distribution built for exactly this — modeling the tail beyond a threshold, not the whole distribution).

Its own threshold, not a generic percentile

"Large" is peril-specific, not one flat dollar figure: PML-based for property, AOA-based for liability, a flat $75,000 for Workers Comp, policy-limit-based for auto — each line's own real per-LOB median large_claim_threshold_usd, computed fresh from the book.

Part A — will it happen (Logistic vs. GBM classifier)

Same drop-and-refit safeguard as frequency: on a thin positive-class sample, a wrong-signed coefficient is treated as noise. 10 property fields and 7 liability fields were dropped this way on the current retrain.

The honest weak point of the whole pipeline

Target prevalence is 1.5% on both groups — 123 positive property rows, 178 positive liability rows, out of 8,199 and 11,801 respectively. That is genuinely too thin a signal for any model to learn a reliable pattern from, and it shows: Property AUC 0.465 (worse than a coin flip), Liability AUC 0.542 (barely better than one). This isn't a code bug — it was investigated directly this project (more features, different model families, rebalancing were all tried) and the ceiling is the data itself: catastrophic/large losses are rare by nature, and 20,000 submissions just doesn't contain enough of them to fit a model that generalizes. See the honest scorecard in §17.

Part B — how big if it does (Generalized Pareto Distribution)

Fit on the excess above each group's threshold, pooled across the group (not per-LOB — too few large-loss rows per individual line to fit a stable tail separately).

A positive shape parameter means a genuinely heavy right tail — the kind of distribution where the biggest few losses matter disproportionately, which matches how large commercial claims actually behave.

08 scoring.py

Loss Propensity Score — combining the three

The three models above don't each produce a score — they produce raw predictions that get combined into one number: an expected loss ratio, then clipped to a 0–100 display score.

The math, in order

exposure_years   = policy_term_months / 12
exposure_units   = TIV/$1M (property)  or  employees (liability)
predicted_frequency = frequency_model.predict(X) * exposure_years * exposure_units

predicted_attritional_severity = severity_model.predict(X)
p_large_loss                   = large_loss_model.predict_proba(X)

expected_large_severity = (this LOB's large-claim threshold + GPD mean excess)
                           * (this submission's severity / this LOB's avg severity)

attritional_freq = max(predicted_frequency - p_large_loss, 0)
total_expected_loss_cost = attritional_freq * predicted_attritional_severity
                          + p_large_loss * expected_large_severity

predicted_loss_ratio_pct = total_expected_loss_cost / premium * 100
loss_propensity_score_v2 = clip(predicted_loss_ratio_pct, 0, 100)   # display only
Why the ratio is scaled by severity, not used flat

expected_large_severity starts from the group's flat threshold-plus-tail figure, then scales it by how this specific submission's attritional severity compares to its line's average — a submission already running hotter-than-average on ordinary claims is assumed to run hotter-than-average on a large one too, clipped to a sane 0.3×–3.0× band so one outlier field can't send it to an absurd extreme.

The raw ratio vs. the display score — this matters for driver attribution

predicted_loss_ratio_pct has no ceiling; loss_propensity_score_v2 is clipped to 0–100 for display. This distinction directly caused and then fixed a real bug — see §13.

Found & fixed — Region/Channel silently contributing zero

pd.get_dummies() only creates a column for a category that's actually present in the current batch, and drops whichever category happens to be alphabetically first among what's present — not necessarily the intended reference level. For a single-row live submission, that meant Region and Channel's real, non-zero fitted coefficients silently never applied — found by testing Channel directly and seeing zero score movement despite a real coefficient. 10 appetite_capacity.py

Appetite / Capacity

Rules-based for the same reason as Bind — no historical outcome to train against, and this is a policy decision with a known structure, not a pattern. Two parts: a hard veto gate, and a 0–100 fit-plus-headroom score for everything that clears the gate.

The hard-rule gate — necessary, not sufficient

4 flags — sanctions/OFAC watchlist, prior insolvency/bankruptcy, restricted operations, region×LOB restriction. Any one True means an automatic 0, confirmed with zero exceptions across all 20,000 rows for that direction of the rule.

Known, disclosed open item — re-verified this session

The 4 flags are confirmed necessary but not sufficient: re-checked directly against the file's own stored appetite_hard_rule_pass_flag, 5,181 of 20,000 rows have all 4 flags clear yet the file still marks them failed — meaning some other, unidentified criterion also drives the original gate that isn't recoverable from the columns available (documented in-code as ~70% of clean-flag rows actually passing). The reconstruction here implements exactly the necessary condition and is honest that it can't fully reproduce the rest — an open item, not silently papered over.

Fit score — a real linear regression

Hard-rule pass, target-growth-segment flag, financial stability score, broker tier, submission completeness, coverage limit, plus a CAT/TIV soft penalty layered on top.

Found & fixed, twice — the CAT/TIV penalty that never fired

First bug: the penalty compared an already-encoded ordinal integer (0–3) against the literal string "Severe" — always False in the real scoring pipeline, so the penalty silently never applied to a single live submission. Fixed with an _is_severe() helper that checks both representations.

Second bug, found fixing the first: that helper used dtype == object as its "is this still text" check — but pandas 3.x's default string columns use a newer Arrow-backed string dtype that isn't object, so the check silently fell through to the numeric branch and crashed comparing text to 3. Fixed with pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(), robust to whichever string backend pandas is running.

Capacity headroom — industry concentration and state reinsurance, tightest wins

Concentration cap is a flat 15% for every industry (confirmed — zero variation across all 10 classes). State headroom is the real per-state average reinsurance binding headroom, and it tells a genuine CAT-exposure story: Oklahoma (−208 pts) and Washington (−172 pts) sit structurally over treaty capacity, not as noise.

Found & fixed — a single submission scored against itself

Industry concentration used to be recomputed from whatever dataframe was passed into score() — correct for a full 20,000-row book re-score, but every single live Submission-tab score calls score() on a one-row dataframe, so that one row's own industry was silently treated as 100% of the reference book every time, for every industry. Forced the headroom component to its floor on almost every live submission. Fixed with a real, fixed INDUSTRY_CONCENTRATION_BY_NAICS constant computed once from the actual 20,000-row book (Manufacturing already sits slightly above the 15% cap at 16.36% — a real, mild constraint).

Found & fixed — one scale couldn't fit two very different ranges

Industry concentration headroom spans roughly −1.4 to +9.1 percentage points across the 10 real classes; state reinsurance headroom spans roughly −208 to +88. One shared multiplier tuned for industry's narrow range made state's much wider range saturate almost immediately — 4 of 31 states (FL, NJ, OK, WA) all floored to the identical score despite genuinely different degrees of constraint. Fixed with two separately-calibrated scales (INDUSTRY_HEADROOM_SCALE=3.0, STATE_HEADROOM_SCALE=0.25) applied before combining, so "tightest constraint governs" actually means something.

Final blend & decision thresholds

appetite_capacity_score_v2 = 0.75 * fit_score + 0.25 * headroom_component   (0 if hard-rule failed)

Decision bands (Within Appetite / Refer–Marginal Fit / Refer–Capacity Constrained / Decline) use thresholds re-calibrated against the real book's own fit/headroom distributions after the scaling fix above changed them — not a reconstruction of the original file's own category logic, which the docstring is explicit isn't recoverable from the fields available.

11 scoring.py / decision_matrix_reference.py

Composite UW Score & risk tiers

uw_score_v2 = 0.30 * appetite_capacity_score_v2
            + 0.20 * bind_propensity_score_v2
            + 0.50 * (100 - loss_propensity_score_v2)

Loss carries half the weight — the single biggest lever on the composite score, matching how underwriting actually prioritizes "will this cost too much" over "will it bind" or "does it fit."

Found & fixed — the most consequential bug this project found

The documented rule is explicit: "Decline: <38, or any hard-rule fail." But tier assignment only ever looked at the blended uw_score_v2 number. Since Appetite is only 30% of that blend, a hard-rule failure (which forces Appetite to exactly 0) could still leave the composite sitting in Standard/High territory if Loss and Bind were good enough to carry the other 70% — e.g. a submission with a sanctions hit but otherwise clean scores landed in "High" with a "rate adjustment" action instead of Decline, directly contradicting the documented rule.

Real impact, measured: 982 of 20,000 real book rows (4.9%) were mis-tiered this way before the fix. Now forced explicitly: risk_tier_v2 = "Decline" if hard_rule_failed else tier_for_score(uw_score_v2). Verified 0 mis-tiers after.

13 driver_explain.py

Driver attribution — "Why This Score"

Every number on the result screen's driver charts is derived from the exact same formulas already used for the real score — this module only decomposes them, it never changes a prediction.

Two different techniques for two different kinds of score

Bind and Appetite are exact linear formulas, so each field's contribution is exact: coefficient × (this submission's value − the real book's average for that field). No estimation, no extra scoring calls.

Loss Propensity has no closed form — it's a blend of 3 separately-trained models, not one linear formula. Each candidate field's contribution is measured by literally re-scoring the submission with that one field swapped to its real book baseline and reading the change in the raw loss ratio — the same perturbation technique used throughout this project's own validation work (§15). This was chosen over an off-the-shelf explainer like SHAP specifically because it naturally handles a composite multi-model score without a new dependency — see §19 for the fuller comparison.

Found & fixed — clipping was hiding real drivers

The comparison originally used loss_propensity_score_v2 — clipped to [0,100] for display. For a submission whose real loss ratio is, say, 224%, the displayed score stays pinned at exactly 100 no matter which single field gets swapped to baseline — almost every field's swap still leaves the ratio well over 100%, so comparing the clipped score made nearly every genuine driver look like it had zero effect, and falsely spotlighted whichever one field's swap happened to cross the 100% boundary as the only "driver." Fixed by comparing the raw, uncapped predicted_loss_ratio_pct instead — verified against a Commercial Auto scenario that previously showed an empty driver list and now correctly shows all 5.

Found & fixed — broker fields silently contributing zero

A live submission's row_dict only carries broker_tier, not the 7 detail fields the Bind formula actually runs on (those are auto-populated, not typed in — §9). Without populating them first, every one of those 7 terms compared a missing value against itself and read as zero contribution. Fixed by calling the same populate_broker_fields() scoring.py already uses, before decomposing.

Book baselines — the "typical submission" every driver is measured against — are real medians/modes computed directly from the source book (TIV $472,000, 31 employees, $70,000 average wage, financial stability 57, etc.), not arbitrary neutral values.

14 narrative.py

Narrative text

The paragraph of English under each result is not an LLM call — every sentence is assembled from real computed numbers using pre-written sentence patterns, chosen by a stable hash of the submission's own scores.

Deliberately decided against an LLM

Discussed directly: cost, added complexity (a new dependency, network calls, latency, non-determinism), against a template approach that's free, instant, and — critically — reproducible: the same submission always reads the same way, which matters for a panel that sits right next to a "Technical & audit detail" drill-down explicitly claiming every figure is deterministic. 2–5 phrasing variants per sentence slot (via hashlib.md5-seeded selection, not Python's random, which reseeds per process) keep different submissions from reading as one template with numbers swapped in, without sacrificing that reproducibility.

Found & fixed — literal asterisks on a client-facing page

**bold** markdown syntax doesn't reliably render nested inside a raw HTML block via Streamlit's unsafe_allow_html=True — it leaked through as literal asterisk characters on screen. All markdown bold syntax was removed from the narrative text entirely; emphasis is handled by the page's own HTML/CSS, not by markdown characters embedded in the copy.

Structure: opening (tier + composite score) → loss propensity (score + plain-English read + top driver) → bind propensity (same shape) → appetite (hard-gate / strong / moderate) → premium (technical vs. quoted, with the rate load spelled out). No redundant closing "recommended action" sentence — that's already the banner's own headline above it.

15 streamlit_app.py

The Streamlit app

One file, three tabs, no separate backend — Streamlit re-runs the whole script top to bottom on every interaction, with @st.cache_resource/@st.cache_data keeping the trained models and the scored 20,000-row book from reloading every time.

What-If Simulator

The live scoring form. Selecting a Line of Business doesn't just grey out irrelevant fields — fields that genuinely don't apply disappear from the screen entirely. Before doing that, every hidden field was checked against the actual scoring code to confirm it truly isn't used for that line (not just assumed) — CAT-relevant fields (flood/EQ/wildfire tier, TIV) are silently defaulted to their old widget defaults for liability submissions rather than shown, since the backend still technically expects them.

Submitting produces: a tier-tinted Underwriting Result banner, a deterministic narrative card, Key Factors chips, 7 KPI cards (composite/loss/bind/appetite scores, technical/quoted premium, probability of large loss), a "Why This Score" panel with three live driver bar-charts (red = pushes the wrong way, green = helps), and a collapsible "Technical & audit detail" drill-down with the full numeric calculation and a reason-codes table.

Portfolio Overview

Filterable (LOB, Broker Tier) view of the whole scored book — submission count, average Loss Propensity, portfolio loss ratio, average premium, a score-distribution histogram, and a matching-submissions table.

Executive Dashboard

5-way filterable (LOB, Occupancy, Risk Tier, Broker Tier, Renewal/New) portfolio view — the full Underwriting Decision Matrix reference table with real book averages per tier, portfolio visuals, a claims overview, and three decision-output queues: Referral Queue, Priority Opportunities, and an Emerging Risk Watch List built from real, disclosed pre-claim indicators (low financial stability, high debt-to-equity, declining revenue, short tenure, safety findings, severe CAT exposure, high competing-quote pressure) — replacing the source file's own frozen prospective_risk_flags column, which can't be regenerated for a brand-new submission.

Visual design pass

Theme-aware throughout (light/dark, reacting to st.context.theme.type) — Inter for UI text, a consistent card system (CARD_BG/CARD_BORDER), and a semantic palette (green/amber/red) matched to tier and driver direction everywhere it appears, not redefined per chart. The result banner is tinted with a translucent wash of its own tier color (not just a colored strip), section headings and the Technical & audit drill-down header are bold and accent-colored rather than default plain text, and Key Factors sits in the same bordered-card treatment as every other section for visual consistency.

Found & fixed — backend Python leaking onto the screen

Three spots used a bare ternary expression statement — st.plotly_chart(fig, ...) if fig else st.info(...) — with the result not assigned to anything. Streamlit's "magic" auto-display quietly renders any unassigned expression's value, so the raw DeltaGenerator object's repr leaked onto the page as visible text. Fixed with proper if/else blocks.

Why the rebuilt (v2) scores don't closely match the file's original stored scores

Loss Propensity v2 correlates with the file's original stored score at only 0.177; Appetite v2 at 0.558. 19 alternatives

Tried and rejected

What this project considered and deliberately didn't do, and why.

GBM as the default over GLM

Not rejected outright — both are trained and raced every time, best-of-deviance/MAE/AUC wins per group per model. GLM wins more often than not on this data (its regularization suits a book this size better than a tree ensemble's flexibility), but nothing is hardcoded to prefer one.

SHAP for driver attribution

Considered directly. Loss Propensity is a blend of 3 separately-trained models with no single closed form — SHAP's fast per-model paths don't reach across that combination in one pass, and standing up a model-agnostic SHAP explainer would have been meaningfully more complexity for a result the existing perturbation technique (already used throughout this project's own validation work) already delivers correctly.

An LLM call for narrative text

Directly discussed: cost per call at scale, added latency, a new external dependency, and — the deciding factor — non-determinism, which conflicts with the "Technical & audit detail" panel's own explicit claim that every figure is reproducible. The deterministic hash-seeded template approach (§14) was chosen instead.

Widening the What-If form's input ranges

Raised, investigated, and reversed on evidence. The hardcoded widget min/max (e.g. wage $46,000–$94,000, employee age 19–66) were checked directly against the real training data's own min/max — and they already match almost exactly. They aren't an arbitrary restriction; they're the honest boundary of what the model has ever seen. Widening them would let a user submit values the model would silently extrapolate on — worse, not better.

Flipping debt_to_equity_ratio's constraint direction

Its dropped, backwards-looking sign was tested directly rather than assumed wrong or right — genuinely significant for Property but with a negligible, likely-confounded effect size (§5), and not significant at all for Liability. Left as dropped.

Fabricating "industry-standard" reweighting

Raised explicitly by the project owner and declined on principle: there's no access to proprietary NCCI/ISO rating tables, and replacing one unverifiable judgment call with another that can't be checked against this book's own real data isn't an improvement — it's a different flavor of guessing. Where a real, data-grounded check was possible (debt-to-equity's sign, a scan for any left-out column with genuine statistical signal), it was run and reported honestly either way — including when the honest answer was "current behavior is already correct, nothing to change."

21 end to end

A brand-new submission, start to end

What actually happens between an underwriter clicking Calculate and a number appearing on screen.

  1. Streamlit collects the form into a one-row DataFrame — only the fields relevant to the chosen Line of Business were ever shown.
  2. score() in scoring.py encodes ordinal tiers, auto-populates the 7 broker-detail fields from Broker Tier, and derives Region from State.
  3. The submission's property/liability group is determined, and the frequency, severity, and large-loss models for that group each run a .predict() — three separate model objects, loaded once and cached, not retrained.
  4. Frequency's raw output is multiplied by exposure-years and the exposure unit to get an actual predicted claim count; large-loss probability and GPD tail severity combine with attritional severity into one total_expected_loss_cost.
  5. Premium is either the real stored value (renewal) or computed fresh from the rate table (new business); loss cost divided by premium gives the loss ratio, clipped to the 0–100 Loss Propensity Score.
  6. Bind Propensity and Appetite/Capacity are computed in parallel — pure closed-form arithmetic, no model call.
  7. The three scores blend into uw_score_v2; the hard-rule gate is checked independently and can force Decline regardless of that blend.
  8. driver_explain.py decomposes all three scores — exact for Bind/Appetite, perturbation-based for Loss.
  9. narrative.py assembles the paragraph deterministically from the finished numbers and drivers — no external call.
  10. Streamlit renders the result banner, KPI cards, driver charts, and the audit drill-down — all from the same result Series computed in step 7, nothing recomputed for display.

Every number reproduces exactly on a second run of the identical input — nothing in this pipeline has a random seed exposed to the outcome once training is finished.
